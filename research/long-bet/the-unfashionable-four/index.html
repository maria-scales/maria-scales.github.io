The Unfashionable Four

Why the A-level combination top universities have always wanted hasn't changed – and why most students don't pick it

TL;DR
  • Four A-level subjects are load-bearing across the 23 STEM programmes in this series that exist as UK degrees: Mathematics, Further Mathematics, Physics, Chemistry.
  • Mathematics is named by 22 of the 23 programmes and is compulsory outright in 20; at Cambridge Computer Science colleges usually want the A* in Mathematics or Further Mathematics. Further Mathematics: named by 2, recommended by 13 – at A*A*A entry, “recommended” is functionally required.
  • Physics is named by 7 of the 10 engineering and physics programmes and compulsory outright in 6, not all of them. Chemistry is named by 6 and compulsory in 4; Biology is named by 4 and compulsory in none – every programme that names it also accepts Chemistry, Mathematics or Physics instead.
  • Economics, Psychology, Business: 0 requirements out of 23. Computing A-level: 0 as well – not one of the 23 asks for it. Mathematics: 22.
  • Fourth subject on top of Mathematics + Further Mathematics + Physics: Chemistry opens all 23. Biology opens 19 and Computing 19 – exactly what the first three open on their own. Only Chemistry adds anything.
  • Sourcing: all 23 rows were read off a live admissions page and link to it. The other 8 of the 31 programme names have no undergraduate degree at any UK university in the ARWU top 100, so there is no A-level requirement to record.

Schools present A-level choice as a menu. Computer Science for the future engineer. Biology for the future doctor. Business for the future entrepreneur. Psychology, Economics, Design Technology – all legitimate, all offered. The message is: pick what's relevant to you.

The admissions data tells a different story. There is a hard core golden standard – four subjects that top research universities have required for decades. Not approximately. Not with occasional exceptions. For decades, across every major STEM field. The menu around them grew. The four didn't move.

The Hard Core Golden Standard
Mathematics  ·  Further Mathematics
Physics  ·  Chemistry
Stable across the 23 programmes that exist as UK degrees, read off their own admissions pages

Here is what the data shows – and why the subjects that feel most relevant are not the ones that are load-bearing.

A note on sourcing before the numbers: every one of the 23 rows below was read off a current admissions page at one of the eight UK universities in the ARWU top 100, and the dataset links to the page each came from. Eight of the 31 programme names in this series have no undergraduate degree at any of those eight – some are taught only at master’s level, some name a department rather than a degree – so the counts below are out of 23, not 31.

Mathematics – named by 22 of 23, compulsory in 20

The baseline. Not a finding – a precondition. A student without Mathematics is not applying to STEM at a top research university. The single programme that does not name it is Civil Engineering at UCL, which names no subject at all: “No specific subjects, but please note the GCSE requirements below if you are not taking A level Mathematics and/or Physics.” Even the exception assumes you have it.

What the data shows beyond this: the A* needs to land here specifically. A*A*A means three A-levels at grades A*, A, A. The natural assumption is to put the A* in whichever subject is strongest. Cambridge CS admissions guidance says otherwise – colleges usually require the A* in Mathematics or Further Mathematics, not just anywhere across the three subjects.

A student can achieve A*, A*, A and still fall short of what Cambridge colleges usually ask for.
If neither Mathematics nor Further Mathematics carries an A*, the condition Cambridge colleges usually set is not met – regardless of total grades. Not every university states the rule this explicitly – but at the most selective programmes the pattern is consistent: the top grade is expected in Mathematics. The golden standard runs all the way down to where the top mark falls.

Further Mathematics – recommended by 13, named by 2

Named in the requirements of 2 of the 23 – compulsory at Cambridge Mathematics, an alternative to Mathematics at Manchester Actuarial Science. Recommended by 13.

Cambridge says "if your school offers it." Oxford says "strongly recommended." Imperial says "recommended but not essential." UCL says "preferred." None of them say required – they don't want to exclude students from schools that don't offer FM. But a student from a school that does offer it and chooses not to take it is at a structural disadvantage across 13 of the 23 programmes.

At A*A*A entry level, "strongly recommended" and "required" are the same thing.
The subject top universities most want is not the one they say they require. This is the first layer of the standard that official admissions guidance obscures.

Physics – the key to the engineering cluster

Named by 11 of the 23. Seven of those are the engineering and physics cluster – Aerospace, Mechanical, Engineering (General), Materials Science, Physics and Astrophysics, where it is compulsory, and Biomedical Engineering, which accepts Biology in its place. The four outside the cluster – Biochemistry, Neuroscience, Earth Sciences and Natural Sciences – name it as one acceptable option among several.

It is not the whole cluster, which is the part the first version of this table got wrong. Three of those ten do not ask for Physics: Chemical Engineering asks for Chemistry, Electrical and Electronic Engineering at UCL asks for Mathematics and prefers Physics or Further Mathematics, and Civil Engineering asks for nothing in particular.

Universities write “recommended” in the edge cases. The core is not ambiguous. Mechanical Engineering at UCL: “Mathematics and Physics required.” Aeronautical Engineering at Imperial: A* in Mathematics, A or A* in Physics. Materials Science at Oxford: Maths and Physics to A-level. Cambridge does not teach these as named degrees – it admits to one Engineering course, and that course requires Mathematics and Physics too.

A student without Physics is not a weak candidate for Mechanical Engineering at UCL. They are an ineligible one.
Physics is the second layer of the standard. It unlocks the largest and most research-active cluster in the dataset. Top universities assume serious applicants already have it.

Chemistry – the subject nobody told you about

Named by 6 of the 23, and compulsory in 4 of them. Biology is named by 4 and compulsory in none: every programme that names Biology also accepts Chemistry, Mathematics or Physics in its place. Most students and parents assume Biology is the relevant choice for anything biology-adjacent. The data does not support this.

The difference is in where each subject's requirements apply.

Chemistry's six programmes – Chemistry, Biochemistry, Chemical Engineering, Neuroscience, Earth Sciences and Natural Sciences – are the ones a student without Chemistry cannot enter, whatever else is on the form.

Biology's four – Biochemistry, Neuroscience, Biomedical Engineering and Natural Sciences – name it only as an alternative, never as the compulsory subject, so Biology is not a gate even in the UK. US universities set no A-level subject prerequisites at all: biology, chemistry and physics are taught from scratch within the degree.

And for Computational Biology specifically – the field that sounds most "biology" – top US universities consider Chemistry the stronger preparation. General Chemistry and Biochemistry appear in year 1 of each of the four US Bioinformatics and Computational Biology degrees checked below. A student with Chemistry arrives prepared for those courses. Biology is taught from scratch regardless.

Verified June 2026:

  • MIT (Course 6-7: CS & Molecular Biology) – biology taught from scratch, no prior Biology required
  • Stanford (BS Biomedical Computation) – all foundational biology, chemistry, physics taken at university
  • UCSD (BS Bioinformatics) – all biology and chemistry are first-year university courses
  • CMU (BS Computational Biology) – Modern Biology, Genomics, Biochemistry, Cell Biology all taught within the degree
The field name says Biology. The degree structure says Chemistry.
Biology A-level is not a global requirement. It is a feature of one country's admissions system. Chemistry is the third layer of the golden standard – and the one nobody states.

The subjects that fill the school menu carry nothing in the admissions data. Four subjects. Each one load-bearing. Each one present at every level – required, recommended, or structurally expected. None of them are surprising. All of them have been there all along.

Biology and Computing are not weaker options. They are not options: each opens exactly the 19 programmes the first three already open. The four they miss – Chemistry, Chemical Engineering, Biochemistry, Neuroscience – all require Chemistry by name.

The golden standard was never hidden. It was always visible in the admissions data. The expanded school menu created the appearance of choice. The data shows the choice was always narrower than it looked.

For Andrey

The research was built for a specific decision. Andrey is preparing to choose his A-levels. The subjects he picks now determine which universities remain open – and which close before he has written a single application.

The answer from 23 programmes read off their own admissions pages, and every layer of this analysis, is unambiguous.

The answer
Mathematics  ·  Further Mathematics
Physics  ·  Chemistry

That combination opens every one of the 23 programmes that exist as UK degrees. There is nothing left for a fifth subject to unlock. At MIT, Stanford, UCSD and CMU it is not a limitation either – it is exactly the profile those programmes are looking for.

The A* goes on Mathematics.

For other students reading this: the data above shows the standard. Whether it applies depends on which universities you are targeting and what you want to study. That decision is yours.
