📈 Data Scientist / ML Engineer
$110K–$200K+ (USA) · £65K–£130K (UK)
The most direct career path. Statistics graduates are valued for their understanding of model assumptions, uncertainty quantification, and inference — skills pure CS grads often lack. Top employers: Google, Amazon, Netflix, Spotify, Two Sigma.
💰 Quantitative Analyst (Quant)
$150K–$400K+ total compensation
Strong demand from hedge funds, investment banks, and proprietary trading firms. Stochastic processes, probability theory, and time series are directly applicable. Top employers: Jane Street, DE Shaw, Citadel, Renaissance Technologies, Goldman Sachs.
🧬 Biostatistician
$90K–$160K (USA) · £50K–£90K (UK)
Clinical trials, epidemiological research, drug development. FDA and pharmaceutical companies require rigorous statistical expertise. Particularly strong pathway from Harvard (BCB track), Oxford, Cambridge. Top employers: Pfizer, GSK, FDA, NIH, academic medical centres.
🔬 Academic / Research Statistician
$80K–$200K (academia) · Higher in industry research
Developing new statistical methodology. Usually requires a PhD. Graduates from ARWU Top 10 Statistics programmes have an excellent pipeline to top PhD programmes (Stanford, Chicago, Berkeley, Columbia).
📊 Actuary
$80K–$200K+ depending on credentials
Risk modelling for insurance, pensions, and finance. Oxford's actuarial science pathway is especially noted. Requires professional exams (FIA, FSA). Stable, well-compensated career with a clear progression ladder.
🏛️ Economist / Policy Analyst
$75K–$150K (government/NGO) · More in private sector
Government statistical agencies, central banks, international organisations (IMF, World Bank). Econometrics skills are central. Princeton ORFE graduates are particularly well-placed. Top employers: Federal Reserve, ONS, OECD, McKinsey Global Institute.
⚙️ Statistical / ML Product Engineer
$120K–$220K (tech industry)
Building recommender systems, A/B testing infrastructure, search ranking, forecasting. Strong statistical foundations are critical for these roles. Top employers: Meta, Airbnb, Uber, Apple, LinkedIn.
🩺 Medical / Epidemiological Researcher
$70K–$140K (research roles)
Designing clinical studies, analysing trial data, public health surveillance. Combination of statistics and domain knowledge. Strong pipeline from Harvard (School of Public Health collaboration) and Oxford.
💡 The bottom line: Statistics graduates sit at the intersection of mathematical rigour and real-world data — arguably the most versatile quantitative degree. Unlike pure maths graduates, statisticians are trained to handle messy, real-world uncertainty. Unlike CS graduates, they understand why models work (and when they fail). This makes them prized in any data-driven field.
💬 Is Statistics different enough from CS to justify as a standalone degree?
Question: CS is becoming general literacy for elite engineers. Statistics is also ubiquitous in every quantitative field. If both are "needed everywhere," does specialising in Statistics still give you a meaningful edge — or do you just end up needing to learn CS anyway?
Answer: The distinction is getting blurrier but it hasn't disappeared. What Statistics gives you that CS doesn't is a deep understanding of uncertainty: how to quantify it, propagate it, and make decisions under it. A CS graduate can implement a model; a statistician can tell you whether that model's confidence intervals are meaningful, whether the experimental design was valid, and whether the p-values are being interpreted correctly. In practice, the highest-value roles (quant research, clinical trial design, causal ML, economic policy) specifically require this statistical rigour. The same pattern as CS applies: Statistics is becoming baseline literacy in data roles, but the people who master it — who understand the theory, not just run R packages — have a compounding advantage. Specialisation on top of Statistics (by domain: genomics, finance, public policy) is exactly what separates researchers from analysts.
