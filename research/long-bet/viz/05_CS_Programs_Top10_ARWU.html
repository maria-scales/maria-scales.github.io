A CS degree from a top university is one of the most versatile qualifications available — the skills transfer across almost every industry. Below are the main paths graduates take, from the most direct to the more unexpected.

Building apps, websites, backend systems, mobile software, databases. Companies like Google, Meta, Amazon, and Microsoft hire directly into engineering roles.

The bottom line

A CS degree from a top ARWU university is essentially a universal passport — one of the few qualifications where the skills apply directly across almost every industry, and where demand consistently outpaces supply. What stays constant everywhere: strong algorithms, systems, and mathematics knowledge opens every door. The specialisation (AI, security, biotech, finance) determines which door you walk through first.

💬 Is CS becoming general literacy — and does that mean you need an extra specialisation on top?
Question

CS is "needed almost everywhere" — doesn't that make it general literacy for elite engineers rather than a specialisation in itself? And if so, won't people need an extra specialisation on top of it to stand out?

Answer

Yes — and it's already happening. CS is following the same path as mathematics. A century ago, knowing calculus and statistics made you exceptional. Now it's a baseline assumption in any technical field. CS is on the same trajectory: modelling problems computationally, writing code, understanding algorithms are becoming expected skills for any serious engineer, scientist, or analyst — not a specialisation in themselves.

The top universities are already signalling this. MIT offers CS & Molecular Biology and CS, Economics & Data Science. Columbia has Computational Biology as a standalone degree. Harvard combines CS with neuroscience in its MBB track. CS is treated as a foundation layer, not a destination.

This makes specialisation cut in two directions:

DEPTH WITHIN CS

AI safety, formal verification, quantum algorithms, systems security. These require serious mathematical maturity on top of general CS and won't be replaced by broad literacy.

CS + A DOMAIN

Computational biology, algorithmic trading, climate modelling, legal tech. The person who wins here isn't the best programmer or the best biologist — it's the person who speaks both languages fluently.

⚠️ The risk in the middle

A general CS degree with no deep specialisation is under genuine pressure — LLMs can produce reasonable code on demand. The generic "software engineer who writes standard apps" role is more vulnerable than "person who deeply understands distributed systems" or "person who can model protein folding."

The most durable profile: CS as infrastructure, plus something genuinely hard on top of it. The key question for planning is not whether to specialise, but which domain to specialise in.
