The Scarcity Ladder

As each layer of data work gets democratised, the bottleneck shifts upward. What was once elite expertise becomes baseline literacy — and a new scarce skill emerges above it.

🔭
1880s – 1940s · Statistical Era
The Age of Small Data
"If you torture the data long enough, it will confess." — Ronald Coase
▼
What happened
Data was precious and scarce — collected by hand from experiments, censuses, agricultural trials. The challenge wasn't handling volume; it was extracting reliable signal from tiny samples rife with noise. Galton, Pearson, and Fisher invented the foundational toolkit: correlation, regression, ANOVA, hypothesis testing, experimental design.
What was abundant
  • Time and patience
  • Physical ledgers & records
  • Domain expertise (biology, agriculture, economics)
What was scarce (= valuable)
Statistical intuition Experimental design Probability theory
Who was elite
Academic statisticians, government actuaries, scientific researchers. A tiny priesthood with access to rigorous mathematical methods.
🖥️
1950s – 1970s · Computing Era
Mainframes & Algorithms
Computers arrived. Very few people could talk to them.
▼
What happened
Computers made statistical calculations feasible at scale — but required programming. Punch cards, FORTRAN, COBOL. Data lived in batch-processed files. The bottleneck shifted from mathematical intuition to: can you write code to run the analysis at all? Turing, von Neumann, Knuth defined this era.
What got democratised
  • Basic statistical calculation (tables replaced by computers)
  • Arithmetic at scale
  • Census & survey processing
What was scarce (= valuable)
Programming Algorithm design Systems architecture
Who was elite
Computer scientists and engineers at IBM, Bell Labs, government agencies. Programming was a deep specialist skill — most people never touched a terminal.
🗄️
1980s – 1990s · Database Era
Spreadsheets, SQL & PCs
Data went into rows and columns. Everyone got a spreadsheet.
▼
What happened
VisiCalc (1979) and Excel (1985) put computation in everyone's hands. Relational databases (Oracle, SQL Server) organised business data systematically. Programming was still specialist, but analysing data in Excel became a mass skill. The bottleneck shifted to: can you design and query databases? Can you build systems that store and retrieve data reliably?
What got democratised
  • Basic arithmetic & tabular analysis (Excel)
  • Statistical reporting
  • Word processing & document creation
What was scarce (= valuable)
Database design SQL & data modelling Enterprise architecture
Who was elite
Database administrators, enterprise architects, ERP consultants (SAP, Oracle). These roles commanded high salaries precisely because the infrastructure was new and complex.
🌊
2000 – 2012 · Big Data Era
The Data Flood
The internet created more data than anyone knew what to do with.
▼
What happened
Google, Amazon, Facebook generated petabytes. Relational databases couldn't cope. Hadoop (2006), MapReduce, Cassandra, Kafka emerged. The challenge: data was now so large and fast that just moving and storing it was a hard engineering problem. "Big Data" became a buzzword. The bottleneck was distributed systems engineering, not analysis.
What got democratised
  • SQL querying (near-universal for analysts)
  • Basic database administration
  • Web development at small scale
What was scarce (= valuable)
Distributed systems Data engineering Scale & reliability
Who was elite
Site Reliability Engineers, Hadoop architects, infrastructure engineers at Google, Facebook, Amazon. These people earned exceptional salaries for keeping systems running at scale.
🧪
2012 – 2020 · Data Science Era
"Sexiest Job of the 21st Century"
HBR 2012. Python + pandas + scikit-learn changed everything.
▼
What happened
Cloud platforms (AWS, GCP, Azure) commoditised infrastructure. Suddenly you didn't need to manage Hadoop clusters — you rented compute. The bottleneck shifted: now the hard part was building models that extracted value from all this stored data. Python's scientific ecosystem exploded. The "Data Scientist" emerged: half statistician, half software engineer, half domain expert (yes, three halves).
What got democratised
  • Infrastructure provisioning (cloud)
  • Distributed storage & processing
  • Basic data pipelines
What was scarce (= valuable)
ML model building Statistical modelling Python + Stats combined Feature engineering
Who was elite
Data Scientists at Google, Netflix, Airbnb. Kaggle grandmasters. People who could go from raw data to a deployed model. Starting salaries surpassed most engineering roles.
🧠
2016 – 2022 · Deep Learning Era
GPUs, Transformers & Neural Nets
Deep learning ate everything. AlexNet → BERT → GPT-3.
▼
What happened
AlexNet (2012) showed deep learning beat everything else on vision tasks. Transformers (2017) did the same for language. AutoML and sklearn made classical ML nearly a commodity. The new frontier: training large neural nets, understanding architecture choices, scaling laws. PyTorch/TensorFlow became the toolkit. The bottleneck shifted to: can you think in tensors and gradient flows?
What got democratised
  • Classical ML (AutoML, sklearn pipelines)
  • Model deployment (MLflow, SageMaker)
  • Data visualisation & BI tools
What was scarce (= valuable)
Deep learning architecture Large-scale training GPU/TPU systems NLP & CV research
Who was elite
ML researchers at DeepMind, OpenAI, Google Brain, FAIR. People who could design and train large models. Compensation reached $1M+ at the frontier. The "AI researcher" premium was extraordinary.
🤖
2022 – Now · LLM Era
Coding Itself Gets Democratised
ChatGPT, Copilot, Claude. The tool that builds tools becomes a tool.
▼
What happened
LLMs made coding itself accessible to non-programmers. A lawyer can now write a Python script. A doctor can build a simple ML model. The floor rose dramatically: tasks that required a junior data scientist in 2020 can now be done by a domain expert with good prompting skills. The bottleneck is shifting — away from "can you write code" toward "do you know what you're actually trying to achieve."
What got democratised
  • Writing code (Copilot, Claude Code)
  • Building standard ML pipelines
  • Data cleaning and transformation
  • Report writing & visualisation
What is scarce (= valuable now)
Domain judgment Problem framing AI system design Evaluating model outputs Uncertainty reasoning
Who is elite now
People who combine deep domain expertise with the ability to direct and evaluate AI systems — not just use them. The bottleneck has moved above the technical layer.

2025 → ? · What Comes Next
The Next Scarcity
If AI automates judgment too, what remains irreducibly human?
▼
What might happen
As AI systems get better at problem framing, the bottleneck may shift further up — toward ethical judgment, accountability, and the ability to navigate ambiguity in contexts where being wrong has real consequences. The skills that are hardest to automate are those requiring trust, responsibility, and long-term relationships. This is an open question — and the first time in history where the answer is genuinely uncertain.
Will get democratised
  • Many knowledge work tasks
  • Standard analysis & reporting
  • Code review & debugging
  • Possibly: basic research synthesis
Hypothesis: next scarce skills
Ethical accountability Institutional trust Deep domain mastery Cross-domain synthesis

So coding is just literacy now — right?
Yes. And it's happening faster than any previous transition.
THE COMPRESSION PATTERN

Every prior skill followed the same arc: scarce → tooled → assumed. Statistics took ~70 years to commoditise (Fisher to Excel). Programming took ~30 years. SQL took ~15 years. Each cycle compressed. Coding is compressing in under 5 — GitHub Copilot, Cursor, Claude already let you ship working software with minimal syntax knowledge.

WHAT BECOMES SCARCE NEXT

"Domain Judgment" is really three things stacked together: knowing what to build (product sense, domain expertise); knowing if it's right (evaluating AI output, catching subtle errors); and knowing why it matters (the scientific or business reasoning underneath).

THE IRONY — SCARCITY RETURNS TO THE FOUNDATION

This brings scarcity back toward the bottom of the stack. Not implementation skill, but the mathematical and causal reasoning that Statistics and CS always claimed to teach. The people who understood the theory — not just the tools — are the ones who can evaluate what the AI produces. A statistician who derived maximum likelihood estimators by hand thinks differently about model output than someone who only ever called .fit(). By the time Andrey graduates university (~2035), the implementation layer will be largely automated. What won't be is knowing whether the answer is actually correct.

Will "Domain Judgment" also be compressed?

That's exactly the right question — and honestly, nobody knows.

Every previous layer was technical: it could be abstracted into a tool because it had a correct answer you could verify. Statistical inference → R. SQL joins → ORM. Neural net training → PyTorch. Each time, the scarce skill was a procedure someone could encode.

Domain Judgment is different because it might not have a verifiable correct answer at the time you need it. A doctor deciding whether to operate, an engineer deciding whether a bridge design is safe enough, a researcher deciding which hypothesis is worth five years of their life — these involve:

Skin in the game — the human is accountable if wrong. AI isn't.
Incomplete information + irreversibility — no clean loss function to optimise against.
Earned trust — institutions and patients trust a person, not a system's output.
Knowing what you don't know — which requires having been wrong before in that domain.

The counterargument is that AI will get good enough that people defer to it anyway, effectively outsourcing judgment even if the AI isn't technically accountable. That might already be happening in radiology and legal research.

So the honest answer: Domain Judgment might compress — but if it does, what replaces it as scarce is something we can't name yet.

"The tools that build tools keep becoming tools. What stays scarce is knowing which problem is worth solving — and having the judgment to evaluate whether you solved it."

📉 The Democratisation Pattern

Every 10–15 years, the technical bottleneck shifts one layer up. Statistics was democratised by software (R, SPSS). Data engineering was democratised by cloud. Classical ML was democratised by AutoML and sklearn. Now coding itself is being democratised by LLMs. The pattern is remarkably consistent.

📈 The Depth Advantage

People who learned a skill before it was democratised carry a different kind of intuition than those who learned it via the tool. A statistician who derived maximum likelihood estimators by hand thinks differently about distributions than someone who calls statsmodels.fit(). This depth compounds over a career.

🎯 What This Means for Choosing a Degree

Don't chase the current scarce skill — by the time you graduate (4 years), it may be commoditised. Instead, study the foundational layer underneath: the mathematics, the probabilistic reasoning, the algorithmic thinking. These are what produce genuine depth, whatever the surface tools look like.

🔁 The Constant: Uncertainty Reasoning

Across every era, one skill has never been commoditised: the ability to reason rigorously about uncertainty. Fisher's p-values, Bayesian posterior distributions, confidence intervals in ML models — the form changes but the underlying skill is the same. Statistics, properly understood, is the long-duration bet.

🌐 The Domain Convergence

Notice that CS, Statistics, and Data Science are converging on the same problem: extracting reliable knowledge from data to make better decisions. The degree label matters less than whether you can move fluidly between the mathematical foundation, the implementation, and the domain application.
